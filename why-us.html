01 — DATA

We drill on data, not hunches.

Most of our leases are backed by well control data or 3D seismic data. Well control means real wells already drilled around our locations, with logs, cores and production history we can study. 3D seismic gives a three-dimensional picture of the rock below the surface, so we can map where the oil-bearing sands sit, how thick they are and where they trap oil.

Our Louisiana locations sit in a field with wells drilled between 1957 and 2011. Our geologists use that history directly:

  • Historical wells in the area have produced from about 35,800 to about 199,500 barrels each from the same sands we target.
  • Some of those wells left oil behind: pay found but never produced, attic oil left updip of older producers, and zones damaged by older completion methods.
  • Our new locations are placed to reach that remaining oil with modern drilling and completion.

02 — STACKED PAY

Multiple pay zones, or we don't drill.

We never take the risk on a single-zone prospect. A location qualifies only when our geology shows at least two, three or four pay zones stacked in the same wellbore. If one zone underperforms, the well still has others to produce from.

03 — TWO WELLS IN ONE

A 100,000-barrel minimum per well.

Suppose a location shows two pay pockets, one of 70,000 barrels and one of 80,000 barrels. Many operators would drill one well and then sell investors a separate offset well into the second, smaller pocket. That spreads investor money across wells that each carry less oil.

We don't do that. Our rule is that it is not economically sound to drill any well with less than 100,000 barrels of oil to recover. So we combine the pay zones into one well of at least 100,000 to 150,000 barrels of estimated oil, and keep the second location as a backup for the same investors. In effect, investors get two wells in one: a primary well sized to clear our hurdle, plus a backup location held for them.

The purpose of the 100,000-barrel minimum is simple: every well we drill should hold enough oil to give investors the opportunity to earn a meaningful multiple on their capital over the life of the well.

07 — CONTROLLED SPENDING

Fixed-price turnkey contracts.

Cost overruns and surprise cash calls are among the most common complaints in oil and gas investing. Meridian structures its programs to avoid them:

  • Fixed-price turnkey drilling. In Venture 2 and the 12 Well Development Program, each well is drilled for a fixed price. If drilling costs more, the drilling company absorbs the difference, not the investors.
  • Escrow and milestone payments. Funds are held on deposit and released only when the drilling or other contracted work is actually done.
  • A known budget. Investors know the cost of each well before they invest.
  • Dry-hole protection in the 12 Well Development Program. If a well is a dry hole, the drilling company drills a replacement well at its own cost.

We value investors' money, and we are careful with every dollar we spend.

TAX ADVANTAGES

Congress encourages domestic drilling.

The expensing of intangible drilling costs has been in the tax code since 1913. For a working-interest investor, these rules can offset a large share of the investment in the first year.

Tax benefit What it means for an investor IRS law
Intangible drilling costs (IDC) Labor, drilling fluids, rig time, site preparation and other non-salvageable costs, usually the largest part of a well's cost, can be deducted 100% in the year incurred IRC §263(c); Treas. Reg. §1.612-4 (optional 60-month amortization under §59(e))
Tangible equipment: 100% bonus depreciation Casing, tubing, pumpjacks, tanks and other equipment can be fully written off in the first year. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act made 100% bonus depreciation permanent for property acquired after January 19, 2025 IRC §168(k), as amended by P.L. 119-21 (otherwise 7-year MACRS under §168)
Depletion allowance Independent producers and royalty owners can generally treat 15% of gross income from production as tax-free, for up to 1,000 barrels per day, subject to taxable-income limits IRC §§611, 613A(c)
Active income treatment A working interest held through an entity that does not limit the investor's liability is not a passive activity, so deductions can offset wages and business income. In Venture 1 this is the Non-Managing General Partner election; the Limited Partner election is ordinarily passive IRC §469(c)(3)
Qualified business income deduction Investors may be able to deduct up to 20% of qualified business income from a working interest IRC §199A

Rules that can limit the benefits

  • Excess business loss limit: caps the business losses that can offset other income in one year (IRC §461(l)).
  • At-risk rules: deductions are limited to the amount the investor has at risk (IRC §465).
  • Prepaid drilling costs: IDC prepaid in one year is deductible only if drilling begins within 90 days after year-end (IRC §461(i)).
  • Alternative minimum tax: excess IDC can be a preference item, with relief for independent producers (IRC §57(a)(2)).
  • Recapture on sale: IDC and depreciation may be recaptured as ordinary income when an interest is sold (IRC §§1245, 1254).
  • Allocation timing: IDC incurred before an investor joins a partnership cannot be allocated to that investor retroactively (IRC §706(d)).
  • State conformity: some states do not follow federal bonus depreciation.

Tax results depend on each investor's own situation. Investors should confirm the benefits with their own CPA or tax adviser before investing.
