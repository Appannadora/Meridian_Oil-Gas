What moves crude oil prices?

A practical overview of supply, demand, inventories, location differentials and uncertainty.

A global market

Crude oil is a globally traded commodity. Prices reflect not only the amount producers supply and refiners demand today but also expectations about future conditions. International events, economic activity, transportation bottlenecks and weather can affect both physical flows and sentiment.

Why inventories matter

Commercial storage is a buffer between production and consumption. Inventories generally increase when supply exceeds immediate demand and decline when users draw down stored volumes. Traders monitor stock changes because they provide a signal about supply-demand balance.

Benchmarks versus realized prices

WTI and Brent are widely followed price references, but individual crude streams can sell at premiums or discounts based on quality, geography, transportation and contract terms. A small onshore producer should not assume that the published benchmark equals the cash received per barrel.

Operating costs are separate

Revenue depends on oil actually measured and sold. Royalty burdens, severance taxes, transport, water handling, repairs, utilities and other expenses can materially change the economics. A favorable benchmark price alone does not demonstrate profitability.

Forecasts are uncertain

Analyst forecasts and scenario assumptions are tools, not guarantees. Oil markets can change quickly. Readers should distinguish historical prices, current market quotations, published outlooks and the realized net price received by a particular seller.
