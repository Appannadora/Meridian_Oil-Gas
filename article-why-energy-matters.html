The role of hydrocarbons in transportation, manufacturing, heating and a changing energy mix.

Energy is not one thing

Energy is consumed in many forms: transport fuels, electricity, industrial heat and the feedstocks used to manufacture products. The U.S. energy mix includes petroleum, natural gas, nuclear energy and renewables. Each source serves different physical needs, and the balance changes with technology, prices and policy.

Beyond the gas station

Refineries turn crude oil into gasoline, diesel and jet fuel. Petroleum also supplies asphalt, lubricants and feedstocks for chemicals, synthetic fibers and many plastics. Natural gas is used for heating, electric-power generation, industrial processes and chemical production. These applications are not interchangeable with electricity in every setting.

A system in transition

Renewable power, batteries, efficiency improvements and electrification are reshaping energy demand. That does not eliminate the near-term role of liquid fuels or molecules used as industrial inputs. The practical challenge is to balance reliability, affordability and environmental impacts while building lower-emission systems.

What domestic development can and cannot do

Domestic production contributes to energy supply, jobs and field-service activity, but no single company or project determines national energy security. Oil is globally traded, and prices respond to worldwide supply and demand. Drilling success and commercial economics remain uncertain at the level of an individual well.
